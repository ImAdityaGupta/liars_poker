1) Advantage-network experiments

Ran many controlled experiments over equal wall-clock budgets. Eventually moved to vast.ai gpu (rtx 3090) to make things quicker.

Tested:

- learning rates
- traversals per iteration
- optimizer steps
- network architecture (width, depth)
- replay ratios and adaptive fitting schedules
- different kinds of infoset encodings
- variations on losses: MSE, Huber, sign-augmented and positive-weighted losses
- raw regret, normalized regret, sign-plus-magnitude and direct-strategy targets

Important findings:

- Positive-weighted MSE consistently beat ordinary masked MSE. `advantage_positive_weight=0.5` became the preferred setting.
- Larger networks helped. On GPU, `2048×2048` was a reasonable winner over `1024×1024`; much larger/deeper networks cost considerably more without clear improvement.
- More optimizer steps can improve approximation per iteration, but often lose per unit wall time because fewer CFR iterations are completed.
- Fixed 100 advantage steps remained a sensible wall-clock baseline.
- Strategy training could be reduced to 50 steps without obvious harm; averaging seems easier than advantage prediction.
- Alternative regret parameterizations were mostly disappointing. Raw positive-weighted regrets remained strongest.
- Richer encodings sometimes helped, particularly on easy games, but the benefit did not generalize convincingly enough to replace the simple encoding (no need for human heuristics).
- Held-out MSE is useful but not sufficient: similar MSE values can induce very different strategies and exploitabilities.
- Current-strategy exploitability is also a poor proxy for final average-strategy quality.
- A cosine learning-rate schedule from `1e-3` to `1e-4` produced a modest improvement, but wasn't crazy good: will need more experimentation, esp. with scaling up.

2) Neural CFR+

Implemented and tested online neural CFR+:

- alternating player updates
- clipped nonnegative cumulative regrets
- linear average-strategy weighting
- frozen regret snapshots during each outer iteration
- separate average-strategy network

Results:

- exceptionally smooth current and average exploitability curves;
- very low clipped-regret validation error;
- current and average strategies much closer in quality than in ordinary Deep CFR;
- slower initial convergence than the best Deep CFR setup, but potentially a more reliable long-run trajectory.

The two big issues with deep cfr are non-monotonicity (sometimes?) and fact that more compute doesn't seem to reliably turn into better policies (plateau is quick). Neural cfr+ seems promising.

3) GPU-native Deep CFR

Reworked Deep CFR substantially for GPU use:

- batched external-sampling traversal; thousands of simultaneous traversal lanes; GPU-native inference during traversal;device-resident replay buffers; fused optimizers; large fitting batches; separate advantage and strategy architectures; exact trainer checkpoint/resume support.

Even on CPU, batched traversal was around an order of magnitude faster than the old recursive implementation. On an RTX 3090, traversal throughput reached millions of generated records per second and ceased to be the main bottleneck.

GPU findings:

- FP32 was at least as good as BF16 for the current network sizes and often faster overall.
- Traversal batch sizes up to 8192 were cheap in memory and extremely fast.
- Fitting became the dominant neural cost.
- Batch size 4096 gave the best wall-clock learning among 4096, 8192 and 16384 despite lower raw sample throughput.
- Wider networks were now affordable, but very large networks still reduced the number of CFR iterations enough to offset their extra capacity.

6) Approximate best responses

At some point exact exploitability becomes infeasible due to size. Implemented a neural DQN-style approximate best responder and calibrated it against games with known exact exploitability (18-claim spec). Key findings:

- Monte Carlo evaluation of a fixed neural responder was highly accurate. With enough games, MC error was very low (to be expected, CLT).
- But sometimes the neural net would just find a suboptimal policy and chill there.
- After 15 minutes, the original DQN responder recovered only about 86% of known exploitability and plateaued with an oracle gap around `0.012`.
- Simple power-law extrapolation predicted the responder's own plateau rather than the true best-response ceiling. It cannot safely repair a weak responder.

This is very annoying. We would like our best responders to be fairly close to the network's actual exploitability, but this isn't the case with DQN. Next tested:
 - whether we can distill into a neural net of current size a good br
 - whether other methods of training can get us closer to a better br. 

7) Exact BR distillation

Directly trained neural net on exact-BR action labels. And we found we can do it, great. Typically our neural policies will have exploitability ~0.1, maybe best case 0.05, but I'd expect this to go up as we scale, so we only need best responder to be within 0.01, maybe 0.005. We could do this: the best exact oracle gap was about 0.0024. Again open question whether this holds up at scale.

We now know the main DQN problem is bootstrapped targets / variance. 

8) Complete-return fitted best response

Implemented a stronger approximate responder based on fully observed terminal returns. So every time responder has to make a decision, we consider all possible legal actions and monte carlo from there to get an idea of which actions are good / bad. Not recursive since we only do this expansion once per decision.

Results were much stronger than DQN:

- after 15 minutes, three seeds recovered about 94–97% of exact exploitability;
- exact oracle gaps were approximately 0.0024–0.0052;
- curves continued improving rather than clearly hitting the DQN plateau;
- performance approached exact-BR distillation quality.

Main conclusion: TD bootstrapping and off-policy target propagation caused much of the DQN failure. Complete terminal returns provide a far better responder-training signal. Also, not too bad for scaling, since scales in number of claims * average length of game, which isn't crazy!

9) PPO responder and future search

Also implemented a PPO best responder with:

- separate actor and value networks;
- clipped PPO updates;
- value baseline;
- entropy regularization;
- role-specific training against a fixed opponent.

Results: better than DQN but not crazy good. So going forwards will probably stick to fitted brs, but may use an ensemble too.

Ok so I figured out some of what was going wrong by training a few different versions of 18-claim games. One is definitely this issue of clipping and taking means - we are getting quite biased estimates of the advantage. Think about a ReLU bias under Gaussian noise. Gemini: 

`When you pass a noisy, unbiased estimate $\hat{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(X, \sigma^2)$ through a non-linear function like $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$, Jensen's inequality guarantees that the estimate becomes positively biased:$$\mathbb{E}[\text{ReLU}(\hat{X})] \ge \text{ReLU}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{X}]) = \text{ReLU}(X)$$The exact analytical expression for the expected value of $\text{ReLU}(\hat{X})$ is:$$\mathbb{E}[\text{ReLU}(\hat{X})] = X \Phi\left(\frac{X}{\sigma}\right) + \sigma \phi\left(\frac{X}{\sigma}\right)$$`

Can try to reduce this via aggregation (essentially reducing the sigma in the above expression) when we are still at a small scale because you expect to see the same infoset a decent amount of the time, but when we scale, you will never see the same hand being dealt to you twice, approximately. So kinda suggests the plus part of CFR+ might be busted?

This aggregation is a big help at mid scale btw, and can see this in the 18 claim experiments. There's definitely something really dumb going on in the fitting / general gpu stuff though, almost certainly - I just haven't been able to dig deep into it yet. One obvious one is you don't want to generate so many traversals that your regret buffer overflows before you fit your regret network! This didn't end up happening for the experiments but we weren't too far off. Also, since we were fitting regret networks on such small amount of data relative to what was being generated, I think that when you clip each record, there's basically no difference between 1024 traversals and 4096 traversals since both lead to way more than 24k or so strategy rows.

In general variance reduction seems like *the key* to scaling, but unsure how this will work going forwards.

Also understood things about how q_t(I, a) and g_t(I, a) worked - the opp reach + chance probabilities and the conditional advantage. Basically exact cfr+ gets these both exactly and adds them to tabular regrets, then clips every iteration, while neural cfr+ essentially doesn't model q_t at all even implicitly [note: except for very niche point that visiting same infostate multiple times means regret network will train on it more, but like this is just increasing the weight here and not changing the target itself], it just has a noisy version of g_t based on Monte Carlo, and *separately* does some rescaling by iteration number t just so that the neural nets don't have to predict a target of a different scale. 

This might be ok if q_t doesn't vary much? But I think it does! So going to test:

0: baseline exact cfr+
1a: exact g_t, estimate q_t by doing traversals K and counting how many times you hit I
1b: exact g_t, set q_t=1 / q_t the same everywhere.
2: exact q_t, estimate g_t via Monte Carlo (I guess same K as before?)
3: estimate of both q_t and g_t as in 1a and 2
4: estimate of g_t and set q_t=1.

In all of the above, we don't have regret or strategy networks - we do this in a tabular way.

	- rescaling the neural cfr+ target by 1/t seems to have dramatically hurt.
	- aggregation before clipping helps a lot (but as we scale up, there essentially is no clipping so this is at best a mid-scale optimisation)
	- it is quite important to model q_t, though it seems hard to get this into a neural form.
	- there is still a late-stage plateau that seems quite hard to get past. not too sure what is causing it / how to fix.
